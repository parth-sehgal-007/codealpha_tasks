How to spot a phishing attack

A short training module: learn the signs, then test yourself with real-looking examples.

What phishing is

Phishing is when an attacker pretends to be someone you trust (a bank, your boss, a delivery company) to make you click a link, open a file, or hand over a password or OTP. It works because it targets people, not software.

Red flags in an email

  • Sender mismatch: display name says "SBI Bank" but the address is support@sbi-secure-login.xyz.
  • Urgency or fear: "Account blocked in 24 hours", "Final warning".
  • Suspicious links: hover before clicking. paypa1.com (a number 1) or amazon.com.verify-now.net (real domain is verify-now.net).
  • Requests for secrets: no real company asks for your password, PIN or OTP by email or chat.
  • Unexpected attachments: .zip, .exe, or "invoice.pdf.exe", macro-enabled Office files.
  • Generic greeting and odd language: "Dear customer", spelling errors, strange formatting.

Fake websites

  • Check the full domain, not just the padlock. HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest.
  • Look for lookalikes: swapped letters, extra words, odd endings (.top, .xyz).
  • Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark for banking and work logins.

Social engineering tactics

  • Authority: "This is the CEO, send gift cards now."
  • Urgency and scarcity: forces you to act before thinking.
  • Curiosity and greed: "You won a prize", "See who viewed your profile".
  • Pretexting: a believable story, such as fake IT support asking you to "verify" your login.
  • Variants: smishing (SMS), vishing (calls), spear phishing (targeted at you), QR phishing (malicious QR codes).

Real-world examples

  • Fake UPI/KYC SMS: "Your account will be suspended, update KYC" with a link to a fake bank page.
  • Business email compromise: a spoofed executive email asks finance to change a vendor's bank account. Losses from such cases run into billions of dollars worldwide.
  • Credential harvesting: a fake Microsoft 365 "password expires" page.

Test yourself

Best practices

  • Stop, look at the sender and link, then decide.
  • Verify through another channel: call the company on its official number.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication and use a password manager (it will not autofill on fake domains).
  • Keep software updated and report suspicious messages to your IT or security team.
  • If you clicked: change the password, tell IT, and monitor your accounts.
